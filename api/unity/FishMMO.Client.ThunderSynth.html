Remarks

Thunder is the channel, heard piece by piece. The return stroke heats every metre of its tortuous channel at once, and each piece bursts outward as a shock that relaxes, within metres, into an N-wave: a sharp rise, a linear fall through zero, a sharp return (Few 1969). Each piece's wave reaches the listener after its own distance over the speed of sound, so a channel kilometres long is heard over many seconds: first the nearest piece's crack, then the roll of the rest, loudest where long straight runs of channel lie broadside to the listener (each piece radiates most at right angles to itself) and their waves arrive together. That is why thunder rolls, and why each clap is different.

The channel. The visible bolt (SkySchedule.BoltPath: its trunk and branches) made tortuous at a finer scale, plus the channel hidden inside the cloud — a strike's in-cloud leader runs kilometres sideways at the cloud's base, which is where the long rumbles come from. All of it is drawn from the strike's seed, so every client makes the same channel.

The air. Each wave falls with distance (spherical spreading, 1/r), stretches a little as it goes, and loses its highs to absorption — its sharp edges rounded, tens of microseconds near and milliseconds far — so the dulling is continuous with distance. The ground under the listener returns each wave a moment later (its image), and the ground, the hills and the cloud scatter it back into a long diffuse tail.

Checked against recordings (2026-10-19). Six field recordings of thunder: energy peaks in the 63–500 Hz octaves (centroids 110–520 Hz), within 20 dB of the peak for 3–18 s, rising over 0.3–3 s for a far roll and at once for a near crack, decaying smoothly. The first version of this put its energy at 16–63 Hz (N-waves several times too long), buzzed (pieces evenly spaced arrive evenly), jumped where distance bands met, and stopped dead when the last piece arrived.

Methods

AddCloudChannel(Channel, Vector3, float, uint)

Adds the strike's in-cloud channel to channel: two to four runs from the top of the bolt along the cloud's base, four to twelve kilometres in all (a cloud-to-ground flash's in-cloud leaders commonly span five to fifteen), wandering as a leader does. Recorded rolls go on pealing ten seconds and more after the first crack; it is these runs, kilometres further off, that do it.

Period(float)

An N-wave's period at a distance: about 2 ms close by, lengthening slowly with the log of the way it has come as the wave stretches. Summed along the channel (the coherent runs add their lows) that puts the thunder's energy in the 63–250 Hz octaves, where the recordings have it.
